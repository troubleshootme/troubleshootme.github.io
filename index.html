🚦 First 90 Minutes: Protect Commitments, Then Pursue Revenue

  1. Triage uncovered shipments by consequence—not by customer noise.

    • Rank pickup deadlines, replacement difficulty, cargo sensitivity and unrecovered delay exposure.
    • Cover production-critical, temperature-sensitive and hard-to-replace movements first.
    • Assign each exposed shipment an owner, a buying ceiling and a customer escalation deadline.
    • Do not let a loud but flexible shipment consume the resources needed for a genuinely critical move.

  2. Separate “not ready” freight from “not covered” freight.

    • Confirm cargo release, written handling instructions, loading method and receiver availability.
    • For imports, distinguish terminal availability, customs clearance and transload completion.
    • For open-deck freight, resolve dimensions, weight, securement, tarping and unloading arrangements.
    • Do not send a truck to solve a readiness problem unless waiting responsibility is agreed.

  3. Refresh offers on identical operating terms.

    • Confirm mileage basis, fuel inclusion, accessorials, truck location, driver hours and appointment feasibility.
    • Obtain multiple qualified offers where time permits.
    • Record why the selected offer wins: complete price, positioning, service reliability or equipment fit.
    • An incomplete low bid is not a negotiating benchmark.

  4. Allocate sales effort after service exposure is contained.

    • First: repeat Southeast freight with confirmed releases and dependable facilities.
    • Second: existing Midwest industrial customers and compatible return loads.
    • Third: specialized or heavy-haul inquiries that fit established carrier capabilities.
    • Prioritize expected contribution per desk hour, not gross revenue per inquiry.

💰 Negotiate the Operating Plan Before the Price

  1. Make deadhead visible.

    • Deadhead means empty repositioning miles.
    • At $6.284/gallon, an illustrative 100 empty miles at an assumed 6 miles per gallon costs about $105 in tractor fuel alone.
    • Driver time, maintenance and any refrigeration fuel are additional.
    • A positioned carrier may offer a better complete price while retaining a healthier operating return.

  2. Exchange concessions for measurable operating value.

    • Test wider loading windows, faster unloading, fewer stops or a destination that supports the carrier’s next move.
    • Ask customers which constraints are mandatory and which are preferences.
    • Request a revised complete offer after changing the operating plan.
    • Do not demand an arbitrary discount when you can remove a real cost.

  3. Set a shipment-specific buying ceiling.

    Maximum carrier buy = customer revenue − other direct costs − required contribution − approved risk reserve.

    • Avoid counting costs twice when they are already included in the carrier’s price.
    • Keep a contingency reserve distinct from an incurred expense.
    • If qualified offers exceed the ceiling, obtain a customer adjustment, revise service or decline the commitment.
    • Do not defend an uneconomic booking merely because the desk has already invested time in it.

  4. Apply fuel changes through the actual contract.

    • Retail diesel has already declined for 14 consecutive days.
    • The futures rebound introduces uncertainty about continued relief; it does not establish that retail prices have turned upward.
    • Use the contractual index and reset schedule for fuel surcharges.
    • Avoid promising future fuel savings in a fixed quote simply because recent pump prices fell.

  • Specialized: protect desk capacity.

    • Prioritize repeat specifications and carriers already qualified for the work.
    • Price unusual handling and equipment commitments explicitly.
    • Require adequate contribution for complexity; a large invoice can still be a poor use of the desk.
