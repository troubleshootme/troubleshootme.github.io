⏱️ First 90 Minutes: Protect the Loads That Can Hurt You

  1. Sort uncovered shipments by latest safe booking time.

    • Put permit-dependent moves, unusual equipment, commodity-sensitive loads and rigid appointments first.
    • Identify the last decision point that preserves preparation, legal transit and customer receiving.
    • Assign an owner and an escalation deadline to each unresolved dependency.

  2. Separate confirmed equipment from conditional availability.

    • Verify the truck’s actual location, preceding delivery, driver HOS (Hours of Service) and preparation requirements.
    • Record whether availability depends on unloading, washout, equipment repair or another shipment’s release.
    • Do not describe a truck as secured while a critical dependency remains unresolved.

  3. Reprice exposed commitments before making new promises.

    • Review uncovered customer commitments whose purchase assumptions are no longer supported by executable offers.
    • For heavy haul and specialized freight, confirm the configuration before comparing prices.
    • Escalate a threatened service commitment while alternatives still exist—not after the preferred truck disappears.

  4. Set a stop-shopping rule.

    • Continue sourcing while the likely savings justify the risk of losing suitable equipment.
    • Stop when further shopping threatens the latest safe booking time or consumes the only viable recovery option.
    • Honor accepted carrier commitments rather than reopening them for a marginal saving.

The objective is contribution from successful execution—not the lowest unaccepted quote.

🚛 Open Deck: Turn Listing Growth Into Qualified Conversations

  1. Flatbed: target freight with a clearly defined operating scope.

    • Ask industrial and building-material customers about uncovered Friday releases, loading flexibility and unloading readiness.
    • Specify trailer type, cargo dimensions, securement, tarping and loading method before requesting bids.
    • Compare identical scopes. An offer excluding tarping or requiring customer unloading equipment is not equivalent to an inclusive offer.

  2. Heavy haul: sell feasibility before speed.

    • Obtain exact dimensions, weight, origin, destination, loading method and required equipment configuration.
    • Confirm route-specific permits, escorts and travel windows before promising departure.
    • Ask whether changing the assigned tractor or trailer would require permit changes.
    • Never assume a missed Friday departure can recover Saturday. Legal movement windows vary by route and load.

  3. Specialized: avoid category-level purchasing.

    • Identify the exact trailer or handling requirement and the operator qualifications.
    • Request bids from carriers that can execute that specific scope.
    • Where only one qualified option exists, disclose the exposure internally before issuing an unconditional customer commitment.

  4. Build a usable backup—not merely a second phone number.

    • Confirm whether the backup carrier has compatible equipment and a feasible arrival time.
    • State clearly whether the carrier is quoting availability or accepting a booking.
    • Do not double-book equipment or imply exclusivity without agreement.

Commercial edge: Customers with complicated freight often value an early, specific warning more than a late promise of cheap capacity.

📅 Friday Month-End: Clarify the Commercial Deadline

  1. Ask what must happen by Friday.

    • Does the customer require pickup, delivery, inventory recognition or completed paperwork?
    • Confirm shipping and receiving hours separately.
    • Obtain order-release cutoffs and after-hours escalation contacts.

  2. Price through realistic equipment release.

    • Include the possibility of waiting, delayed unloading and weekend occupancy in the operating plan.
    • Agree on detention, layover, rescheduling and cancellation treatment before exposure develops.

  3. Remove speculative reload savings.

    • Validate unloading time, remaining legal hours, trailer preparation and the next pickup window.
    • Treat a missed reload as a possible consequence of the first shipment—not an unrelated surprise.

Useful negotiation question: “If receiving moves to the next operating day, who authorizes the change and how is the equipment commitment handled?”
