⏱️ First 90 Minutes: Buy Certainty Where Failure Is Expensive

  1. Rank uncovered loads by consequence and recovery time.

    • Put temperature-sensitive, production-critical, permit-dependent and rigid-appointment shipments first.
    • Assign each load an owner, latest safe booking time and recovery option.
    • Shop flexible freight longer only while a credible alternative remains executable.

  2. Verify the truck behind the offer.

    • Confirm actual location, preceding unload, assigned equipment and HOS (Hours of Service).
    • Identify remaining dependencies: washout, permit issuance, uncertain unloading or driver assignment.
    • Ask the carrier: “What must happen before this truck can legally and physically make pickup?”
    • Apply carrier identity and authority checks even when recovery is urgent; independently verify unexpected contact or payment changes.

  3. Set the buying ceiling before negotiation becomes emotional.

    • Use: Maximum carrier commitment = customer revenue − other direct costs − required contribution − risk allowance.
    • Include known repositioning, handling and third-party expenses.
    • Do not use an unconfirmed reload or hoped-for detention recovery to make an otherwise unprofitable purchase appear acceptable.

  4. Stop shopping when delay becomes more expensive than the likely saving.

    • Compare plausible savings with the probability-weighted incremental recovery cost of waiting.
    • Consider service failure separately: a small purchasing saving may not justify jeopardizing a production appointment.
    • Escalate an over-ceiling executable offer early, while the customer still has choices.

🧩 Partial Freight: Protect the Entire Route’s Contribution

The fastest-growing listing category can also consume the most dispatch attention per dollar of revenue. Distinguish LTL (Less Than Truckload) terminal-network service from shared-truck partials before pricing.

  1. For shared-truck partials, establish a total-charge floor.

    • Include incremental carrier cost, additional distance, stop time, handling, preparation, administrative cost and risk.
    • Account for the opportunity cost of occupied linear feet, weight capacity and lost scheduling flexibility.
    • Remember that an appointment can consume more useful capacity than the freight’s physical footprint.

    Decision test: Accept the shipment only when its added revenue exceeds its added costs and opportunity cost while preserving existing service commitments.

  2. Test the complete sequence—not just geographic proximity.

    • Confirm loading order, unloading access, stacking restrictions, securement and cargo compatibility.
    • Check whether a “nearby” stop creates a missed receiving window or an extra overnight hold.
    • Obtain shipper approval for shared-truck service and any handling or transfer arrangements.

  3. For terminal-network LTL, control shipment-description exposure.

    • Verify dimensions, weight, classification and packaging.
    • Confirm residential delivery, limited access, liftgate, appointment and other applicable charges.
    • Do not transfer shared-truck per-mile assumptions into a network-LTL quote.

  4. Use a two-load example to expose false profit.

    • A partial may fit physically but push the original shipment beyond its receiving cutoff.
    • Its incremental revenue must then cover waiting, rescheduling and any displacement of the truck’s next assignment—not merely the detour.
    • Reject consolidation that adds revenue while reducing total-trip contribution.

⛽ Negotiate Carrier Economics Without Damaging Trust

  1. Separate fuel burden from fuel direction.

    • Diesel is $6.317/gallon. Futures weakness may eventually ease retail costs, but timing and pass-through remain uncertain.
    • Apply the contract’s fuel index, observation period and adjustment formula.
    • Confirm whether the carrier offer is all-in or separates linehaul and fuel; avoid counting the same adjustment twice.

  2. Offer operating improvements carriers can actually value.

    • Aggregate averages rarely persuade a carrier facing a difficult appointment or expensive repositioning.
    • Present executable alternatives and clear shipment facts.
    • Recognize that carriers may prioritize payment certainty and reduced dwell over a nominally higher rate with uncertain execution.

    • Marine oil-transport costs indicate upstream fuel risk, not domestic truck-rate inflation.

    • Use news to manage exposure—not manufacture urgency.
