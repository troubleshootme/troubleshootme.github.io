⏱️ First 90 Minutes: Spend Attention Where Failure Is Expensive

  1. Rank uncovered shipments by consequence and decision deadline.

    • Put production-critical deliveries, temperature-sensitive cargo, fixed appointments and permit-dependent moves first.
    • Assign a latest safe booking time, recovery owner and customer escalation point.
    • Shop flexible freight only while a credible recovery option remains.

  2. Replace indicative offers with operating facts.

    • Confirm actual truck location, trailer configuration, preceding unload and HOS (Hours of Service).
    • Ask whether availability depends on an uncertain release, washout, permit, delivery or road approach.
    • Identify which carrier offers are firm and when they expire.

  3. Run a controlled buying test.

    • Give qualified carriers identical commodity, weight, dimensions, appointments and accessorial requirements.
    • Request the required-service offer and, separately, a customer-approved flexible alternative.
    • Record why carriers decline. Repeated appointment refusals point to a different problem than repeated equipment shortages.

  4. Set customer pricing from executable cost.

    • Use: Maximum carrier commitment = customer revenue − other direct costs − required contribution − internal risk allowance.
    • Keep the risk allowance separate from incurred expenses.
    • Make unresolved scope and quote expiration explicit.

Illustrative decision rule: If waiting may save $50, but creates a 20% chance of $250 in incremental recovery expense, the expected recovery cost already equals the saving—before considering service damage. Those are hypothetical decision inputs, not measured market probabilities.

Practical implication: A slightly cheaper truck is not cheaper if its availability depends on an unload nobody can confirm.

🗺️ Southeast Sales: Turn Seasonal Interest Into Bookable Freight

  1. North Carolina → Atlanta: qualify the shipment before pitching capacity.

    • Use the Raleigh-area market as a prospecting starting point, then establish the actual farm, packhouse or warehouse origin.
    • Confirm product readiness, loading hours, shipment frequency, receiving appointments and cargo value.
    • Seek a specification-complete trial shipment rather than promising a broad harvest-season solution.

  2. Sweet potatoes: obtain written handling instructions.

    • Confirm temperature, ventilation, loading condition, cleanliness and prior-cargo restrictions.
    • Do not apply generic cold-produce settings or assume pre-cooling is appropriate. Sweet potatoes are chilling-sensitive.
    • Determine whether active refrigeration is required and whether the proposed equipment can maintain the shipper’s instructions.

  3. Atlanta → Orlando: sell reliable replenishment with explicit exit economics.

    • Confirm live-load versus drop-trailer requirements, unloading expectations and appointment flexibility.
    • Ask carriers to explain their positioning plan; do not assume a Florida reload exists.
    • Offer a flexible-window alternative only when an actual carrier concession supports it.

  4. Connect the legs only after compatibility is proven.

    • A produce arrival does not automatically become suitable distribution capacity.
    • Verify trailer acceptance, prior cargo, cleaning, unloading completion, remaining driving hours and next pickup cutoff.
    • Price each leg to stand alone until the connection is booked. Never subsidize a committed movement with a speculative northbound reload.
  • Customer conversation: “We can price the required appointment and an approved flexible option separately. Any saving will be tied to confirmed equipment and an executable schedule.”
  • Carrier conversation: “Which change improves your economics most: less empty travel, faster loading or a wider receiving window?”

Why this works: Specific operating choices reduce uncertainty. Unsupported claims about a “tight market” invite resistance and become difficult to defend when customers receive competing offers.

🛠️ Specialty Freight: Sell Scope Control Before Price Certainty

The widest advertised-to-paid differences sit where equipment and shipment details matter most. Specialized shows a 24-cent paid premium, heavy haul 20 cents, and flatbed 6 cents. These are aggregate differences—not automatic quote adjustments.

  1. Specialized: build a qualification gate.

    • Establish trailer type, loading method, operator qualifications, handling requirements and applicable insurance.
    • Obtain a scope-complete carrier offer before issuing a firm customer quote.
    • Separate standard service from engineering, rigging or other third-party requirements where applicable.

  2. Heavy haul: confirm movement feasibility.

    • Obtain exact dimensions, weight distribution, axle requirements, origin and destination access.
    • Verify route feasibility, permits, escorts and jurisdiction-specific movement windows.
    • Distinguish a budget estimate from a carrier-confirmed movement subject to identified approvals.

  3. Flatbed: compare equivalent work.

    • Confirm tarping, securement, loading and unloading equipment, and site restrictions.
    • Do not treat a lower offer as comparable when it excludes required labor or equipment.
    • Prospect repeatable industrial shipments where complete specifications make purchasing more dependable.

  4. LTL/partial: identify what service is actually being purchased.

    • For LTL (Less Than Truckload) terminal-network service, verify classification, dimensions, weight, handling and accessorials.
    • For shared-truck partials, verify usable space, cargo compatibility, route deviation and delivery sequence.
    • Compare total delivered cost and service exposure—not the mileage average alone.

Competitive edge: A broker who catches an omitted permit, unsuitable trailer or unloading requirement before booking protects both contribution and customer trust.
